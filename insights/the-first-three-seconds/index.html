The first three seconds of a meeting

Gaurang Misra · Insights

Every interaction is partly decided before anyone speaks. In the first few seconds of walking into a room, joining a call or sitting down across a table, the other person makes four quick reads about you, almost entirely below conscious thought.

The four reads

  • Status: where you sit in the hierarchy of this room. Above them, below them, equal.
  • Threat: whether you could cost them something: time, face, their position.
  • Value: whether you could give them something worth their attention.
  • Compliance: whether you'll go along with them, or whether they'll need to go along with you.

None of this is decided by what you say. It's decided by how you enter, how still you are, where your eyes go, how quickly you respond, and the first sound of your voice.

Attention is decided early

People have limited attention, and they ration it fast. In the opening minute or so of a meeting, the room decides how much of that attention you'll get. The goal isn't to be liked. It's to be registered as someone worth listening to.

Small things that shift the reads

  • Stillness. Settle before you speak. Shuffling papers, adjusting your chair and hurried greetings all read as lower status.
  • A grounded voice. Let your first sentence end with a falling tone, not a rising one. It sets your register for everything after.
  • Steady eye contact. Hold the gaze of the person you're addressing, then move it deliberately. Darting eyes signal threat or anxiety.
  • A slightly later response. Answering instantly signals eagerness. A short, comfortable pause before you reply signals that you're considering, not reacting.

Why it matters for leaders

Senior people often assume their title does this work for them. It does, until they walk into a room where everyone has a title. Then the reads happen again from scratch, and the person who carries the room is rarely the most senior. It's the one who was read correctly in the first three seconds.
