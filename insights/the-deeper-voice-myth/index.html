The deeper-voice myth

Gaurang Misra · Insights

When I started making reels for my businesses, the camera showed me something I couldn't hear in person. I sounded young. Not wrong, just light. The kind of voice people like but don't follow.

So I did what every speaking coach recommends. I dropped my voice. Deeper, slower, heavier. It sounded like authority. It also cost me the one thing that mattered: people stopped relating to me.

Why "speak deeper" fails

A put-on voice is a performance, and performances don't last. In my experience it holds for about four minutes. Then you get absorbed in what you're saying, the effort slips, and your real voice comes back, now with an audible switch that tells everyone the first version was an act.

Even while it holds, it does damage. A voice that's all weight and no warmth signals distance. People respect it from across the room and keep their real thoughts to themselves.

Authority isn't one voice

What I eventually understood is that the people with real authority don't have a single impressive voice. They have range, and they use it on purpose:

  • A rising tone when they want input. It invites people in.
  • A neutral tone for facts. It signals competence without pressure.
  • A falling tone for decisions. It closes the question.

Most people use only one of these, and usually by accident: the rising tone, at the end of everything, turning every decision into a request for permission.

Tuning, not pretending

Over twelve months I rebuilt my voice around that range. Today I don't have a deep voice, a sharp voice and a normal voice. I have one voice that adjusts to whoever I'm talking to. With some people a calm, falling delivery lands best. With others, warmth first and weight later.

The adjustment isn't about becoming someone else. It's about noticing how the other person listens, and meeting them there.

How to start

  1. Take five sentences you say often at work: a decision, a request, a refusal.
  2. Say each one three ways: rising, neutral and falling at the end.
  3. Record them and listen back. Notice which version you actually use, and which one the sentence needed.

Then practise until the right one comes out without thinking. That's the difference between conditioning a voice and performing one.
