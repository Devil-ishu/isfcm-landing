Your words are the least important part of what you say.

Gaurang Misra · Insights

I make mistakes when I speak. Wrong word, half-finished sentence, a bit of grammar that wouldn't survive an English teacher. Most people never notice. Not because they're being kind, but because they aren't listening to my words in the first place. They're following my tone and what I'm trying to convey.

That's true of you too. And it's why so much communication training misses the point.

What people actually process first

Before a room has processed a single sentence of yours, it has already formed a view. How certain you sound. Whether you rushed. Whether you looked like you needed them to agree. That view arrives first, and everything you say afterwards gets filed under it.

So when you say something sharp in a hesitant voice, people hear hesitation. When you make a good point too quickly, people hear nerves. The words were right. The verdict was already in.

Why polishing doesn't help

The standard advice is to polish: speak in complete sentences, learn better vocabulary, remove the pauses, neutralise the accent. All of it works on the words, which is the part people pay least attention to.

Worse, polish often costs you something. Perfectly constructed sentences sound rehearsed. A flawless delivery can feel like a performance, and people trust performances less than they trust someone who sounds like they mean it.

Influence can be built even with rawness. What matters is speaking in the right manner, to the right person.

What to work on instead

  • The end of your sentences. Most people lift their pitch at the end, which turns statements into requests. Let decisions fall.
  • Your pauses. A pause after an important point gives the listener time to agree with it. Rushing past it tells them it wasn't important.
  • Your stillness. Fidgeting, nodding along and over-explaining all read as effort. Calm reads as authority.
  • The listener. People hear things in their own manner, not yours. Before you choose your words, notice how this particular person takes things in.

A simple test

Record yourself explaining a decision you've made. Then play it back with the sound off and watch only your face and body. Then listen with your eyes closed and ignore the words, just the rise and fall of your voice. Ask yourself one question each time: would I follow this person?

If the answer is no, the fix isn't a better script.
