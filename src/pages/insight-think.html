"Let me think about it" is a verdict, not a question.

Gaurang Misra · Insights

You've had the meeting. It went well. They nodded, asked good questions, said it was interesting. Then, at the moment of decision: "Let me think about it."

Most people treat this as a pause. They follow up, send more information, wait. Usually the answer never comes.

What it actually means

"Let me think about it" is rarely about needing time. It's a polite way of saying the decision has already been made, and it isn't yes. Somewhere in the conversation the other person concluded that saying no to you would be easy. They just didn't want to say it in the room.

And that conclusion usually wasn't about your offer. It was about you.

Every objection is a test

When someone raises a concern ("it's expensive", "the timing isn't right"), they're checking one thing: whether you hold. Most people don't. They rush to reassure, speed up, explain more, offer a discount before it's asked for. Each of those moves tells the other person that your position is negotiable, and that your confidence was a performance.

It's one of the patterns I see most often in diagnosis calls. Strong meetings, then silence. The moment price comes up, the person speeds up and over-explains. Their words defend the price. Their tone apologises for it.

How to stop hearing it

  • Slow down at the moment of pressure. When an objection comes, pause before answering. A calm pause says the question doesn't threaten you.
  • Answer once. State your position clearly, with a falling tone, and stop. Don't keep adding reasons. Each extra reason sounds like doubt.
  • Make the decision easy, not the "no". Close by asking for the decision directly. A clear question is harder to deflect than an open-ended "what do you think?"
  • Notice earlier. The verdict forms long before the end. Watch for the moment their attention drops, and address it then, not at the close.
The deal that dies at "let me think about it" usually died twenty minutes earlier.
